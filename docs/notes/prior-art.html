Prior Art: Where Each Idea in the Framework Already Exists

The owner asked "has this been discovered before?" and "nobody has really done anything like this before?" This file maps each component of the framework to existing work. The point is not to diminish the framework. Arriving at these positions independently is a good sign. The point is to answer the question honestly and give the owner the reading list Gemini did not.

The Fog Front

"You can only see the layer directly in front of you."

  • Kant's phenomena vs. noumena: we know only how things appear to us, never things in themselves.
  • McGinn's cognitive closure: some truths are inaccessible to a mind of a given architecture, the way calculus is closed to a dog.
  • Noam Chomsky's "problems vs. mysteries" distinction.
  • Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974).
  • In physics, the horizon idea appears as effective field theory: each energy scale has its own description, and higher-energy structure is invisible from below.

The Infinite Box Cascade (regress of creators)

  • Bostrom's own paper discusses nested simulations and stacked levels.
  • The classic "who made God?" regress appears in Hume, Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779), and in Bertrand Russell, Why I Am Not a Christian (1927).
  • Aquinas's first-cause argument is an attempt to stop the regress; the owner instead lets it run into the Un-Concept, which is the Neoplatonic move.

The Central Finite Curve and the hourglass

  • The name "Central Finite Curve" comes from the TV series Rick and Morty (season 5, 2021), where it denotes the bounded set of realities in which a particular condition holds. The owner adopted the name for their own structure.
  • The shape (many branches below, convergence above) matches Plotinus's emanation scheme in reverse and resembles a Porphyrian tree.

"Going up is cleaner, going down is messier"

  • Philip Anderson, "More Is Different" (Science, 1972): each level of complexity has its own laws not deducible in practice from the level below.
  • Effective field theory and the renormalization group (Wilson, 1970s): coarse descriptions become simpler because irrelevant microscopic detail washes out.
  • Daniel Dennett, "Real Patterns" (1991): higher-level patterns are real and more compressible.
  • Erik Hoel, "causal emergence" (2013 onward): macro-scales can carry more causal information than micro-scales.

This is the idea Gemini called an "inversion of Hoffman." It is not; Hoffman's interface theory is about perception being fitness-tuned, and it is fully compatible with emergence.

The apophatic gates

  • Gate 1 (all-good god vs. suffering) is the problem of evil: Epicurus (via Lactantius), Hume's Dialogues, J. L. Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence" (1955). Responses: Alvin Plantinga's free will defense (1974); John Hick's soul-making theodicy. The evidential version: William Rowe (1979).
  • Gate 2 (no one can speak for the ineffable) is the via negativa itself and also appears in Maimonides' negative attributes and in Zen's "if you meet the Buddha on the road, kill him."

Elimination as method

  • Sherlock Holmes's "eliminate the impossible" (Doyle, 1890) and Popperian falsification (Karl Popper, Logik der Forschung, 1934).
  • The known flaw: elimination requires an exhaustive partition. Philosophers call the risk "false dichotomy" or, for scientific theories, the "underdetermination of theory by evidence" (Duhem, Quine) and the "problem of unconceived alternatives" (Kyle Stanford, 2006).

Multi-universal evolution

  • Lee Smolin, "cosmological natural selection" (The Life of the Cosmos, 1997): universes reproduce via black holes and their constants evolve.
  • Teilhard de Chardin's noosphere and Omega Point (1950s), and Frank Tipler's Omega Point (1994), for consciousness climbing toward a cosmic endpoint.
  • Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit (1807): Spirit coming to know itself through stages of history.

The "efficiency trap" (Google and schools make us less capable)

  • Plato, Phaedrus: Socrates argues writing will weaken memory.
  • Nicholas Carr, "Is Google Making Us Stupid?" (2008) and The Shallows (2010).
  • Sparrow, Liu, Wegner, "Google Effects on Memory" (Science, 2011). Replications have been mixed.
  • Ivan Illich, Deschooling Society (1971), for the anti-school position.

Un-learning and observing one's own mind

  • Vipassana and Zen "witness consciousness"; Samkhya's Purusha observing Prakriti.
  • Metacognitive therapy (Adrian Wells) and ACT's "cognitive defusion."
  • Zen's shoshin (beginner's mind), Shunryu Suzuki (1970).

Fused hardware / cognitive expansion

  • Andy Clark and David Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998).
  • J. C. R. Licklider, "Man-Computer Symbiosis" (1960).
  • Ray Kurzweil, The Singularity Is Near (2005).
  • Current brain-computer interface work: Neuralink, Synchron, Precision Neuroscience.

Waking up from the Un-Concept (the owner's self-refutation)

  • Buddhist and Advaita traditions already hold that awakening reveals the previously unknowable, which is the owner's point that the fog moves.
  • In philosophy of science, this is the pessimistic meta-induction: every past "final theory" was superseded, so the current horizon is probably not final.

What is genuinely the owner's own

Combining these into one tree with a knowability horizon and an elimination procedure is a personal synthesis, and a reasonable one for someone thinking this through from scratch. Nothing in it is a priority claim against working scientists or philosophers, and nothing in it needs protecting. The valuable next step is not publication but reading the primary sources above and seeing which branches survive contact with them.
